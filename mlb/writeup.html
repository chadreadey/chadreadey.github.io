How much does the count move the strike zone?

A model of the umpire's ball/strike call, trained on 1.06M called pitches and tested on 708K from seasons it never saw.

Every taken pitch in baseball ends in a judgement call: ball or strike. I wanted to know how predictable that call is, what drives it, and where it stops being predictable. So I revisited an old class project and rebuilt it from the ground up on Statcast: every pitch an MLB umpire called from 2022 to 2026, 1.77 million in all.

The setup

The question. Will the umpire call this taken pitch a strike? Only called strikes and balls are kept, so every row is a judgement call.

The data. Statcast, pulled with pybaseball, regular season only. Besides where the pitch crossed the plate, Statcast records the top and bottom of each batter's strike zone, which makes it possible to ask whether a pitch was in that batter's zone.

The test. Train on 2022–24 (1.06M pitches), test on 2025–26 (708K). Umpires, pitch mix and the zone itself drift from year to year, so the model is judged on seasons it has never seen. About a third of called pitches are strikes, so "always ball" scores about 67%; that is the floor.

The model

A logistic regression, kept deliberately simple so every term is readable. Location is rescaled so each batter's rulebook zone becomes the same square: a pitch at 3.4 feet is a strike to a tall hitter and high to a short one, and the model should know that. On top of that come batter handedness, the count, and count × location interactions (17 terms in all).

Raw location and count already get you most of the way. Measuring location against the batter's own zone adds a point of accuracy and, more usefully, better-calibrated probabilities, which is what the demo displays.

Finding 1: the error lives at the edge

Far from the zone, the model is right more than 99% of the time. Within an inch outside the rulebook zone, umpires call about half of pitches strikes, and the model is right only about 65% of the time.

Nothing in location and count separates the calls in that band. That is where umpire judgement, catcher framing and pitch type would have to come in.

Finding 2: the count moves the zone about 20 points

Take a right-hander and a pitch exactly on the outside edge at mid-zone height. The model gives a 72% chance of a called strike at 0‑2, 88% at 0‑0 and 92% at 3‑0. Same pitch, same spot, about 20 points apart depending on the count. Umpires widen the zone when the pitcher is behind and tighten it when the batter is in a hole.

Try it

The interactive demo runs the same fitted model in the browser. Drag the pitch onto the corner, switch between 0‑2 and 3‑0, flip the batter's side or zone size, and watch the 50% line move.

What's next

  • Pitch type, movement and catcher. A gradient-boosted model with those features, aimed at the edge band where the logistic model runs out.
  • Umpires. Statcast doesn't record the home-plate umpire. Joining it from the MLB Stats API would allow per-umpire zones.
  • ABS. How much did ABS change the edge? With the automated ball-strike challenge system in play in 2026, the 0–1 inch band is where to look: compare how often those pitches go each way before and after.
