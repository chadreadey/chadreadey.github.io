How much does the count move the strike zone?

A model of the umpire's ball/strike call, built on 1.06M called pitches and tested on 708K from seasons it never saw.

Every taken pitch in baseball ends in a judgement call: ball or strike. I wanted to know how predictable that call is, what drives it, and where it stops being predictable. So I revisited an old class project and rebuilt it from the ground up on Statcast: every pitch an MLB umpire called from 2022 to 2026, 1.77 million in all.

The setup

The question. Will the umpire call this taken pitch a strike? Only called strikes and balls are kept, so every row is a judgement call.

The data. Statcast, pulled with pybaseball, regular season only. Besides where the pitch crossed the plate, Statcast records the top and bottom of each batter's strike zone, plus each pitch's type, speed, spin and movement, and who was catching.

The test. Everything is built on 2022–24 (1.06M pitches) and tested on 2025–26 (708K). Umpires, pitch mix and the zone itself drift from year to year, so the models are judged on seasons they have never seen. About a third of called pitches are strikes, so "always ball" scores about 67%; that is the floor.

Two models

A readable baseline. A logistic regression with 17 terms. Location is rescaled so each batter's rulebook zone becomes the same square: a pitch at 3.4 feet is a strike to a tall hitter and high to a short one. On top of that come handedness, the count and count × location interactions.

A gradient-boosted model. LightGBM on the same zone and count inputs, then adding the pitch itself: type, speed, spin, movement and pitcher hand. It is fit on 2022–23, with 2024 split by game for early stopping and probability calibration. A third version adds a catcher framing score.

Most of the gain is flexibility, not new information: on exactly the same inputs, the boosted model beats the logistic one by half a point and cuts log loss by 9%. The pitch features add a little more. The demo runs the third row.

Finding 1: the error lives at the edge

Far from the zone, both models are right more than 99% of the time. Within an inch outside the rulebook zone, umpires call about half of pitches strikes. That band is where the boosted model earns its keep, and it is still the hardest place to call.

Finding 2: the count moves the zone about 19 points

Take a right-hander's four-seam fastball to a right-handed batter, exactly on the outside edge at mid-zone height. The model gives a 69% chance of a called strike at 0‑2, 83% at 0‑0 and 88% at 3‑0. Same pitch, same spot, about 19 points apart depending on the count. Umpires widen the zone when the pitcher is behind and tighten it when the batter is in a hole.

Finding 3: four-seamers get the fewest edge strikes

Straight from the data: within an inch of the zone edge, on either side, umpires called 61% of four-seam fastballs strikes in 2025–26, against 66% of sinkers. Changeups (62%) sit near four-seamers; cutters, sliders, curveballs and sweepers fall in between (64–65%).

Finding 4: catchers differ, but framing didn't help the forecast

I built a framing score for each catcher: called strikes above what location and count predict, shrunk toward zero for small samples. Catchers do differ. From the 10th to the 90th percentile, the score runs from about −9 to +9 called strikes per 1,000 called pitches. But adding it to the model changed nothing on 2025–26. The effect is a percent or two of calls, mostly away from the edge, and a 2022–24 score may not carry forward. A better framing measure is an open question.

Try it

The interactive demo runs the LightGBM model in your browser with ONNX Runtime Web. Pick a pitch type and pitcher hand, drag the pitch onto the corner, flip between 0‑2 and 3‑0, and watch the 50% line move.

What's next

  • Umpires. Statcast doesn't record the home-plate umpire. Joining it from the MLB Stats API would allow per-umpire zones.
  • ABS. How much did ABS change the edge? With the automated ball-strike challenge system in play in 2026, the 0–1 inch band is where to look: compare how often those pitches go each way before and after.
  • Framing, done better. A per-season or pitch-level framing measure, to test whether catcher skill can be predicted ahead of time.
